Act I — Order-to-Cash as the enterprise lifeline

O2C is the only process that turns revenue into cash. Every leak in it is permanent.

The two ringed stages are the control points. Everything else is throughput.

Upstream leak

Credit exposure you should never have carried. Prevention.

Midstream leak

Money already taken off your invoice by the customer. Recovery.

Downstream leak

Aged balances written off because nobody could prove the claim. Loss.

Open here. Most of the room automates process steps. Frame O2C differently: it is the only end-to-end process whose output is cash, so a leak anywhere is not a delay — it is a permanent loss booked against margin.

Act I — why these two disciplines

Deduction management and credit management are the same signal read at two different times.

Deduction management

after the sale

The money is already gone. You are reconstructing what should have been paid from a POD, a contract clause, a promo accrual — and then proving it well enough to ask for it back.

  • Recovers cash that has already left
  • Produces the evidence a customer will accept
  • Feeds root cause back to Billing so it stops recurring

Credit management

before the sale

The money hasn't gone yet. You are deciding how much exposure this customer earns, from payment drift, dispute behaviour and external signals.

  • Prevents exposure you'd later write off
  • Prices risk into terms rather than blocking orders outright
  • Turns a blunt credit hold into a graduated response

The link is the reason this is one product and not two

A customer filing repeated invalid shortage claims is not only a deduction problem — that behaviour is a credit signal, and the strongest one you have, because it is first-party and current where a bureau score is third-party and stale. Recoup's Closed-Loop Risk Mesh exists to arbitrate exactly that: when forensics says gaming and the credit file says healthy payer, something has to weigh the two and record why it landed where it did.

This is the slide that earns the architecture. If they accept that deduction behaviour is a credit signal, they will accept why a supervisory agent arbitrating conflicting positions is the right shape — rather than two independent workflows.

Crestline Grocery is the live example: ~$48M/yr, a healthy payer on paper, and repeatedly filing invalid shortage and pricing claims.

Act I — the handover to Act II

Proving one $21,300 line means assembling evidence from six systems that were never designed to agree.

SAP S/4HANA

Billing document, sales order, pricing conditions, AR sub-ledger.

3PL / WMS

Proof of delivery — signature, timestamp, piece count.

TPM

The approved promotional accrual the claim is measured against.

Document repository

Contract clauses, damage photos, carrier reports.

Remittance / EDI

What the customer said they were deducting, and why.

Credit bureau

External risk signal, third-party and lagging.

Why RPA stalls here

There is no fixed path. Which evidence settles the question depends on what the claim asserts — a shortage needs a POD, a pricing chargeback needs a contract clause, a promo overclaim needs an accrual. You cannot enumerate the branches in advance, and the documents are half unstructured.

Why unconstrained LLMs are worse

The output of this process is a dollar figure sent to a customer who will contest it. A model that is confidently wrong about $21,300 does more damage than the analyst backlog it replaced — and cannot be audited afterwards.

So the interesting question is not can an agent do this. It is what has to be true before you'd let one.

Land the closing line and pause. This is the hinge of the whole session. Everything in Act II is an answer to "what has to be true before you'd let one".

Act II — picking the right tool

Three automation shapes, and the one property that decides between them.

Recoup's position

Agentic for the open-ended part — deciding what evidence would settle this claim and going to get it. Deterministic for everything that touches a number, a verdict, or the outside world. The split is not a compromise; it is the design.

The failure mode to design against is not a model that can't reason. It's a model whose reasoning reads convincingly and cites nothing — and an operator who ships it because it sounded right.

The row that matters is "explains its reasoning". For RPA that's a gap. For an agent it's a hazard — fluent narrative is exactly what makes an unverified answer dangerous. Everything in the next ten slides is machinery for not trusting the narrative.

Act II — the architecture in one idea

Three planes, and a strict rule about what each is allowed to own.

Reasoning plane
src/agents/

Owns — planning, retrieval intent, explanation, handoff narrative.

Never owns — dollar math, verdicts, ERP credentials, approvals.

Data plane
src/mcp/ · src/adapters/

Owns — governed source access behind typed ports; credentials.

Never owns — autonomous decisions.

Control plane
src/core/ · src/services/ · src/audit/

Owns — rules, amounts, routing, approval state, hash-chained audit.

Never owns — free-form model judgment.

The boundary is compiled, not documented

dependency-cruiser enforces port purity: src/core/ has zero imports from SAP, synthetic sources or adapters, and the runtime cannot import datagen/. Adapters return canonical entities only. A violating import fails npm run verify — you cannot merge the shortcut.

One entry point, parse then dispatch

invokeServiceTool(name, input, ctx) rejects anything not in the whitelist, Zod-parses the input against that tool's schema, and only then calls the handler. There is no free-form tool-execution path anywhere in the codebase (I-15).

src/services/serviceLayer.ts

Permission is separate from availability

evaluateToolPermission() returns allow approval_required deny. external_correspondence denies outright unless the actor is a human: principal holding the send_email capability. Every draft_only and financial call returns approval_required by classification, not by remembering to check.

src/services/permissionEngine.ts

Amounts are clamped at the tool boundary

draftRebill forces its amount through clampToComputedDelta() — a recovery can never exceed the computed delta (I-6). proposeHold goes further: it re-runs computePartialHoldAmountSplit() and throws if the caller's numbers disagree with the core.

The design idea worth stealing: classify tools by risk and side-effect class as metadata, then derive permission and exposure from the classification. Adding a tool without a class is a type error; you can't forget to secure it.

proposeHold throwing on a mismatch is the sharpest example of "code computes every dollar" — the model may propose a split, but the core recomputes it and rejects disagreement.

MCP used as least privilege, not as a plugin bus.

Not sap.query. The agent never gets a general-purpose door into the ERP — it gets scoped questions it is allowed to ask.

Client-side filter

createMCPToolStaticFilter trims tools/list before the model ever sees it. Tools outside the allowlist are not hidden — they are absent from the context.

Server-side visibility

The facade re-checks visibility === "mcp" on every call, then re-runs the permission engine. A client that forges a call to an internal tool is refused at the handler boundary.

Per-request evidence scope

Headers x-recoup-query-answer-scope and x-recoup-credit-risk-answer-scope carry a base64url-encoded, Zod-parsed {selectedLineId, recordIds}. The request pins the allowed evidence — not the model's request.

In production the MCP server never leaves the process

RECOUP_MCP_URL is deliberately unset, so createMayaMcpGateway() starts an ephemeral authenticated loopback server on port 0 with a generated per-run token, connects, and tears it down in a finally. Source credentials live in the gateway and never enter agent context.

src/agents/mcpGateway.ts · src/mcp/server.ts

The transport is real MCP

StreamableHTTPServerTransport, stateful sessions, bearer auth compared with timingSafeEqual, JSON-RPC 2.0 error envelopes, a secret-free /healthz, and per-tool annotations (destructiveHint: false, openWorldHint: false). Missing token config fails closed.

Anticipate the question: "why MCP at all if it's in-process?" Two answers — the protocol gives you a real capability boundary with tool filtering and annotations for free, and the same surface can be exposed to an external client tomorrow without re-plumbing the permission model.

The evidence-scope header is the subtle one. The model can ask about any record; the request decides which records the tool is permitted to read. Worth saying twice.

Act II — memory

Memory as a typed, inspectable artifact — not hidden prompt state.

Eleven categories, three trust levels

session_state · workflow_state · case_state · transaction_state · evidence_refs · approval_records · audit_refs · connector_state · compaction_summaries · artifact_refs · agent_handoff_packets

Each record is trusted, semi_trusted or untrusted. Handoff packets arrive semi_trusted by default. Every record must carry at least one recordId.

src/memory/schema.ts

PII cannot be persisted — enforced at parse time

The Zod schema walks the payload and rejects emails, phone numbers, sk- keys and secret-shaped field names. This isn't a scrubber that runs before writing; a record containing PII fails validation and never becomes a record.

Compaction is deterministic

No summarisation model call. compactMemoryRecords() preserves objective, category, next step and the deduped record IDs, and takes the worst trust level of its inputs — compaction can never launder an untrusted record into a trusted summary.

The recall path is treated as untrusted input

Recall is the classic injection vector: something written earlier gets replayed into a later prompt. isTrustedMayaCaseRecallRecord() gates every read on category, trust level, scope, ID shape and status, and rejects any record ID matching an unsafe pattern — @, bearer, secret, token, sk-, phone shapes.

src/memory/session.ts

The two points to make are schema-level PII rejection (validation, not sanitisation — the distinction matters) and treating the memory read path as an injection surface. Most teams guard the write and trust the read.

Act II — retrieval

Semantic search is allowed to find evidence. It is not allowed to introduce it.

How the retrieval actually runs

An OpenAI vector store holds the POD, contract and promo dossiers. buildEvidenceQuery() composes a scoped query — customer: … deduction: … scenario: … record: … — and posts to /v1/vector_stores/{id}/search.

Then the interesting part. mapSearchResults() cross-checks every hit against a checked-in manifest and discards any result that doesn't match on all six of:

  • customer_id
  • scenario_type
  • source_table
  • record_id
  • documentType
  • the full record-ID set

src/adapters/openAiVectorStore.ts · config/openAiEvidenceVectorManifest.ts

Why this matters more than retrieval quality

A hallucinated citation is worse than no citation, because it survives review. Pinning hits to a manifest means the failure mode of bad retrieval is we found nothing rather than we cited something that doesn't exist. Recall degrades; integrity doesn't.

Retrieval is additive, never load-bearing

Vector search failures are caught per line and fall through to an empty set. The deterministic evidence path still produces the answer — semantic retrieval widens what the investigator can reach, it is never the thing the verdict rests on.

Provisioned, versioned, verifiable

The seed-42 corpus is provisioned by script and validated by a readiness check, so the manifest and the store cannot silently drift apart.

npm run provision:openai-evidence-vector-store

Best single line of the session, and worth delivering slowly: semantic search may find evidence, never introduce it. Most RAG discussions are about recall; this one is about integrity, and that's the conversation an audit function will care about.

Act II — the pattern worth taking home

Most agent systems trust the trace. This one audits it.

The inversion: the deterministic answer is built first. The live agent run is not the source of the answer — it is a proof obligation the answer must discharge before it may be shown. When the trace can't prove itself, the answer is blocked.

  1. Build the deterministic answer from cited records. Can't build it → block.
  2. Run the agent with a forced toolChoice and the evidence scope pinned in the request headers.
  3. Inspect the trace for a successful MCP source-read proof. Missing → retry once with an explicit validationRetryReason injected, then merge the runs.
  4. Assert a hook receipt showing the Forensics Investigator → Recovery Drafter handoff actually fired. Absent → block.
  5. Return the deterministic answer, annotated with modelExecution metadata.

src/services/forensicsQuerySession.ts · creditRiskQuerySession.ts

Failure is a named state, not a degraded answer

live_openai_agents · blocked_live_agent_trace · blocked_missing_credentials. There is no silent fallback to a worse answer — the surface tells you which one you got.

Receipts are extracted off the wire

registerRunHookAuditReceipts() subscribes to all five SDK lifecycle hooks and pulls provenance out of the actual tool arguments and results — toolInputSelectedLineId, toolOutputPrimarySourceSystem, toolOutputSapEvidenceRecordIds, toolOutputSourceFreshness. Each receipt is Zod-validated and must cite at least one record.

src/services/conductor.ts

Run control fails closed

maxTurns, retryCap and per-phase token budgets are loaded from the database. Unset or invalid → the run is blocked and /healthz returns 503. There are no hard-coded defaults to quietly fall back to.

If they remember one slide, make it this one. The generalisable pattern for any regulated automation: compute the answer deterministically, run the agent for coverage and reach, then require the trace to prove specific things happened before the answer ships.

Step 4 is the sharp end — an assertion that a specific handoff fired. It means "the agent said it did the work" is never sufficient.

be ready for these

Close — what to take back to your own builds

Three patterns that transfer to any automation you'd trust with consequences.

Code computes, the model decides

Split the work by consequence, not by capability. The model chooses what to look at and what to do next; deterministic code produces anything a human will act on. Then make that split a test, so it can't erode under delivery pressure.

Tools are a permissioned registry, not a grab bag

Classify every tool by risk and side-effect class, derive exposure and permission from the classification, and route all calls through one parse-then-dispatch entry point. Adding a tool without a class should be a type error.

Treat the trace as evidence, not narration

Don't ask whether the agent says it did the work. Assert that specific receipts exist — this handoff fired, this source was read — and block the output when they don't. A trace you never check is decoration.
